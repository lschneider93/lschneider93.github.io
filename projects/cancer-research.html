Experience · Biostatistician I · Myriad Genetics · Jul 2022 – Oct 2025

Statistical analyses for cancer and genetic testing research.

Three years answering questions for lab, clinical, and research teams. Five of the questions are below, each with how I approached it and what it showed. Study names and numbers are left out on purpose, except for the published paper. The charts are illustrative, not study data.

Research question 1

Do patients with a known cancer type score differently on the tests we provide than patients without cancer?

Patients with prostate, breast, and other cancers arrive with a diagnosis already made. Do their test scores differ from patients without a cancerous tumor? Can we measure that difference, and how do we quantify it?

Explore first

Put the two groups side by side and look at the shape of their scores before fitting anything.

Then confirm

Logistic regression predicting cancer status from the test score, adjusting for patient characteristics. The model turns "looks higher" into an odds ratio with a confidence interval, and says how much of cancer status the score explains.

What it showed: the cancer group scored higher on average, and the model put a number on the shift. Reported with its limitations, so the team could decide what to study next.

Research question 2

Can we measure the effect of educating healthcare providers on genetic testing, and how will that influence genetic testing rates?

OB/GYN clinics were taught about hereditary cancer screening and testing. Did the share of eligible patients who got tested change from before the education to after? And did the change differ by provider type, physician versus advanced practice provider (APP)?

Published result

In the published study, the share of patients who met testing guidelines and completed genetic testing went from 16.0% (189 of 1,184) to 34.2% (439 of 1,285). Read the paper on PubMed ↗

Explore first

Compare the before and after rates overall, then split them by provider type. The chart below is illustrative, not study data

Then confirm

Conditional logistic regression with site as the strata term. Clinics differ in patient mix and in how they work, so the before and after comparison has to stay within each site.

What it showed: testing rates rose after the education, and the gain was larger for advanced practice providers.

This question became the published study

Online screening and virtual patient education for hereditary cancer risk assessment and testing

Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2025 · co-author

Peer-reviewed
  • Question: can an online screening tool and virtual education help community OB/GYN clinics find more patients who qualify for hereditary cancer testing, and get them tested?
  • Method: a before-and-after study at five practices, comparing the eight weeks before the tools with the eight weeks after, using conditional logistic regression stratified by site.
  • Result: more eligible patients were identified, more were offered testing, and the share who completed testing went from about one in six to one in three.
  • My role: co-author, contributing to the statistical analysis.
  • Also in the pipeline: additional manuscripts were in draft or under review when the role ended in a company layoff in October 2025.

Research question 3

Given an assumed hazard ratio, how many patients does a study need to reach 80, 85, 90, or 95% power?

Before a study starts, the team needs an enrollment target. The answer depends on the effect they expect (the hazard ratio), the power they want, and the model they will use. The table shows how fast the number grows as the expected effect shrinks.

Patients to enroll for a two-sided 0.05 log-rank test with equal groups, assuming half of enrolled patients reach the event. Events needed is half the number shown. Change the event rate or the design and the numbers move, which is the point of showing the team a grid instead of one figure.

How the answer changes with the model: a survival model sizes on a hazard ratio. A logistic model on a yes or no outcome sizes on an odds ratio instead, and the same study can need a different number of patients depending on which question it is really asking.

Research question 4

How do gene expressions and genetic mutations differ between patients who have a certain cancer and patients who do not?

Across tested patients, which mutations and expression patterns show up with which cancers, and how strong is the pattern?

How: association analysis across the tested population. Survival models where the outcome was time to an event, logistic models where it was a yes or no.

Why it mattered: the goal behind this work was to find cancer earlier, and once a cancer is found, to point to the best treatment.

Research question 5

When the lab gets new machinery, how does it compare to the old?

How much of the variation in results comes from the machine, the operator, and the reagents, and does it stay inside the allowed threshold?

How: variance-components and mixed-effects models, with acceptance limits set before the data came in.

What the team got: a go or no-go answer with the evidence attached.
