Experience · Biostatistician I · Myriad Genetics · Jul 2022 – Oct 2025

Statistical analyses for cancer and genetic testing research.

Three years answering questions for lab, clinical, and research teams. Study names and numbers are left out on purpose, except for the published paper.

Question
Can a study answer what the team is asking, and can the data behind it be trusted?
What I did
Designed analyses, planned sample sizes, built reproducible R workflows with data-quality checks, modeled outcomes with survival, logistic, and mixed-effects models, reviewed colleagues' work, and reported results to researchers and clinicians.
What it showed
Studies that started with a defensible plan, datasets that were clean before anyone modeled them, and one peer-reviewed publication.
Why it matters
In genetic testing, a wrong answer reaches a patient. The statistics have to hold up.

The kinds of questions I answered

Four that came up again and again. Each one is a real type of request, written without the specifics.

Is the new machine as reliable as the old one?

When the lab added equipment, I measured how much results varied between machines, operators, and reagent lots, and whether that variation stayed inside the allowed threshold.

How: variance-components and mixed-effects models with acceptance limits set before the data came in.

The team got: a go or no-go answer with the evidence attached.

How many patients does this study need?

Using prior history and an expected positive rate, I worked out the sample size needed to detect the effect with enough power.

How: power analysis under stated assumptions, with a check on how the answer moves when those assumptions move.

The team got: a study sized to answer its question before it started.

Does the evidence back up the claim?

Claims about certain markers, and how specific tumor types respond to them, needed checking against the data.

How: re-analysis with survival models and logistic regression, reported with its limitations.

The team got: confirmation or a correction, documented either way.

Can we trust this dataset?

Linking patient information to clinical tests and results, without introducing bias along the way.

How: reproducible R workflows that flag discrepancies, duplicate records, inconsistent values, and unreliable inputs before anything is modeled.

The team got: an unbiased dataset and a record of every check that was run.

A published example

Online screening and virtual patient education for hereditary cancer risk assessment and testing

Obstetrics and Gynecology, 2025 · co-author

Peer-reviewed
  • Question: can an online screening tool and virtual education help community OB/GYN clinics find more patients who qualify for hereditary cancer testing, and get them tested?
  • Method: a before-and-after study at five practices, comparing the eight weeks before the tools with the eight weeks after, using conditional logistic regression stratified by site.
  • Result: more eligible patients were identified, more were offered testing, and the share who completed testing went from about one in six to one in three.
  • My role: co-author, contributing to the statistical analysis.

What this shows

  • Study design and power: sizing a study so it can answer its question.
  • Modeling that fits the data: survival, logistic, and mixed-effects models chosen for the question, not the habit.
  • Data quality before modeling: reproducible checks, and a record of them.
  • Review and communication: other people's work checked, and results explained to the people who act on them.
