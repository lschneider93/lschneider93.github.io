How many patients does a study need?

Pick the size of the effect you hope to find. See how many people it takes to find it. Each calculator starts from a real published study.

At Myriad Genetics I ran power and sample size calculations like these for cancer studies. Every formula here follows the NCSS PASS documentation and is tested against its worked examples.

Time until something happens

Cox regression asks if a factor changes how soon an event comes, such as cancer returning. The effect is a hazard ratio.

Worked example from published research

A tumor gene score and prostate cancer coming back

Cuzick J, Swanson GP, Fisher G, et al. Prognostic value of an RNA expression signature derived from cell cycle proliferation genes in patients with prostate cancer: a retrospective study. Lancet Oncol. 2011;12(3):245-255. PubMed 21310658 · free full text

The team scored 366 tumors removed by surgery, then tracked whose cancer came back.

Hazard ratio
1.89 per 1-point rise in the score (95% CI 1.54 to 2.31)
Event rate
36% of the cohort had the cancer return (biochemical recurrence) by 10 years
SD of the score
0.70 assumptionThe paper gives the middle half of scores, -0.30 to 0.64, but no SD. For a bell-shaped score, SD is about that width divided by 1.35.
R² with other factors
0 assumptionThe 1.89 comes from a model with the score alone, so nothing else shares its signal.
Alpha and power
0.05 two-sided, 90% power assumptionThese are design choices, not results. Both are common standards.

Formula and source

Hsieh and Lavori (2000), as documented in the PASS chapter "Cox Regression" (chapter 850).

D = (z1-α/2 + z1-β)2 / [ (1 - R2) σ2 B2 ]    N = D / P

  • D: events needed. N: patients needed.
  • B: log of the hazard ratio for a 1-unit rise in the predictor.
  • σ: SD of the predictor. A yes or no factor split 50/50 has σ = 0.5, which gives Schoenfeld's (1983) formula.
  • R²: how much of the predictor the other covariates explain.
  • P: share of patients who have the event.

Power for a given N: Φ( √(N · P · (1 - R²)) · σ · |B| - z1-α/2 ).

Checks: the code matches the chapter's Example 1 power table, Example 2 (N = 106), and Example 3 (N = 212).

Sources: PASS chapter, Cox Regression (PDF). Hsieh FY, Lavori PW. Sample-size calculations for the Cox proportional hazards regression model with nonbinary covariates. Control Clin Trials. 2000;21:552-560. Schoenfeld DA. Sample-size formula for the proportional-hazards regression model. Biometrics. 1983;39:499-503.

Yes or no outcomes

Logistic regression asks if a factor changes the chance of an outcome, such as getting cancer. The effect is an odds ratio.

Worked example from published research

A genetic risk score for breast cancer

Mavaddat N, Michailidou K, Dennis J, et al. Polygenic risk scores for prediction of breast cancer and breast cancer subtypes. Am J Hum Genet. 2019;104(1):21-34. PubMed 30554720 · DOI

A score built from 313 DNA markers was tested in 10 prospective studies (11,428 women with breast cancer, 18,323 without).

Odds ratio
1.61 per 1 SD rise in the score (95% CI 1.57 to 1.65)
Baseline chance
1.7% assumptionThe paper reports 3,215 new breast cancers among 190,040 UK Biobank women, which is 1.7%. Using that as the chance for a woman with an average score is a planning choice.
R² with other factors
0 assumptionTreats the score as unrelated to the other factors in the model.
Alpha and power
0.05 two-sided, 90% power assumptionThese are design choices, not results. Both are common standards.

Formula and source

Hsieh, Bloch and Larsen (1998), as documented in the PASS chapter "Logistic Regression (Legacy)" (chapter 860).

Predictor on a number scale (normal):

N = (z1-α/2 + z1-β)2 / [ P0 (1 - P0) B2 ]

  • P0: chance of the outcome at the average value of the predictor.
  • B: log of the odds ratio for a 1 SD rise in the predictor.

Yes or no predictor:

N = [ z1-α/2 √( P̄ (1 - P̄) / R ) + z1-β √( P0(1 - P0) + P1(1 - P1)(1 - R) / R ) ]2 / [ (P0 - P1)2 (1 - R) ]

  • P0, P1: chance of the outcome without and with the factor.
  • R: share of people who have the factor. P̄ = (1 - R) P0 + R P1.

Other covariates: divide N by (1 - R²), where R² is how much of the predictor the other covariates explain.

Checks: the code matches the power values in the chapter's Examples 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 to five decimals, and the odds ratio in Example 3. PASS prints N = 981, 335 and 3,326 in Examples 2 and 4, where power is a hair under 90%. This page rounds the formula up, so it gives 982, 336 and 3,327.

Sources: PASS chapter, Logistic Regression (Legacy) (PDF). Hsieh FY, Bloch DA, Larsen MD. A simple method of sample size calculation for linear and logistic regression. Stat Med. 1998;17:1623-1634.

How well a score sorts people

AUC is the chance a score ranks a person with the disease above a person without it. A coin flip scores 0.50. A perfect test scores 1.00.

Worked example from published research

The Gail model for breast cancer risk

Rockhill B, Spiegelman D, Byrne C, Hunter DJ, Colditz GA. Validation of the Gail et al. model of breast cancer risk prediction and implications for chemoprevention. J Natl Cancer Inst. 2001;93(5):358-366. PubMed 11238697 · DOI

The model's 5-year risk estimate was checked in 82,109 women from the Nurses' Health Study.

AUC
0.58 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.60), reported as the concordance statistic
Without cancer per case
59.6, from 1,354 breast cancer cases among the 82,109 women
Null AUC
0.50 assumptionA coin flip. It is the usual "no skill" mark to beat.
Type of score
Continuous assumptionThe risk estimate is a number on a smooth scale, so the continuous-data formula applies.
Alpha and power
0.05 two-sided, 90% power assumptionThese are design choices, not results. Both are common standards.

Formula and source

One ROC curve tested against a null AUC, as documented in the PASS chapter "Tests for One ROC Curve" (chapter 260).

N+ = [ zα √V(AUC0) + zβ √V(AUC1) ]2 / (AUC1 - AUC0)2

For a continuous score the chapter uses the Hanley and McNeil variance, with θ as the AUC and R as the number of people without the disease per person with it:

V(θ) = θ / [ R (2 - θ) ] + 2θ2 / (1 + θ) - θ2 (1 + R) / R

  • N+: people with the disease. People without: R × N+.
  • zα uses α/2 for a two-sided test.

Power for a given N+: Φ( [ |AUC1 - AUC0| √N+ - zα √V(AUC0) ] / √V(AUC1) ).

Checks: every worked example in the chapter uses rating-scale data (the Obuchowski and McClish variance). The code matches those: Example 1 power table, Example 2 (N+ = 1,582, 381, 85) and Example 4 (162 and 243). The chapter prints no worked example for continuous data, so that variance is checked against the Hanley and McNeil (1982) standard error instead.

Sources: PASS chapter, Tests for One ROC Curve (PDF). Hanley JA, McNeil BJ. The meaning and use of the area under a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Radiology. 1982;143:29-36. Obuchowski NA, McClish DK. Sample size determination for diagnostic accuracy studies involving binormal ROC curve indices. Stat Med. 1997;16:1529-1542.

These are planning formulas. They assume the model fits and use large-sample math, so treat the answers as a starting point. A real study plan also adds room for dropouts.
