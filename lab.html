How many patients does a study need?

Given certain assumptions.

Power and sample size calculations are often required in cancer studies. The formulas come from the NCSS PASS documentation.

The equation

N = (z1−α/2 + z1−β)2P (1 − R2) σ2 (ln HR)2

The top part divided by (1 − R2) σ2 (ln HR)2 is the number of events needed. Events = N × P, so patients = events ÷ P.

z1−β = √N · P · (1 − R2) · σ · |ln HR| − z1−α/2power = Φ(z1−β)

The same equation, solved for the power term. N × P is the number of events.

N
number of patients
P
share of patients who have the event
σ
SD of the score
HR
hazard ratio per 1-unit rise in the score
R2
overlap between the score and the other factors
α
chance of a false alarm, 0.05 two-sided
Power
1 − β, the chance of finding a real difference
z, Φ
a point on the standard normal curve, and the area to its left
Source

Hsieh and Lavori (2000), as given in the PASS chapter "Cox Regression" (PDF). A yes or no factor split 50/50 has σ = 0.5, which gives Schoenfeld's (1983) formula.

Hsieh FY, Lavori PW. Sample-size calculations for the Cox proportional hazards regression model with nonbinary covariates. Control Clin Trials. 2000;21:552-560. Schoenfeld DA. Sample-size formula for the proportional-hazards regression model. Biometrics. 1983;39:499-503.

Numbers from this paper

Cuzick J, Swanson GP, Fisher G, et al. Prognostic value of an RNA expression signature derived from cell cycle proliferation genes in patients with prostate cancer: a retrospective study. Lancet Oncol. 2011;12(3):245-255. PubMed 21310658 · free full text

Hazard ratio
1.89 per 1-point rise in the score
Event rate
36% of patients had the cancer return by 10 years
SD of the score
0.70 assumptionThe paper gives the middle half of scores, -0.30 to 0.64, but no SD. For a bell-shaped score, SD is about that width divided by 1.35.
R² with other factors
0 assumptionThe 1.89 comes from a model with the score alone.
Alpha
0.05, two-sided

What each input does

  • Hazard ratio: as the hazard ratio increases, fewer events are needed for the same power. A bigger effect is easier to see. See it below
  • SD of the score: as the SD increases, fewer events are needed. Patients differ more, so the difference between them is easier to see. See it below
  • Event rate: the lower the share of patients who have the event, the more patients are needed. Only events carry information. See it below
  • Power: 80%, 85%, 90%, 95%. As we increase power we need more events, because we want to be more confident that a real difference is found. See it below
  • R² with other factors: held at 0. We assume the score is independent of the other factors.
  • Alpha: held at 0.05, two-sided.

The equation

N = (z1−α/2 + z1−β)2P0 (1 − P0) (1 − R2) (ln OR)2

This is for a score on a number scale. P0 (1 − P0) is largest when P0 is 50%, so that is where the fewest people are needed.

z1−β = √N · P0 (1 − P0) · (1 − R2) · |ln OR| − z1−α/2power = Φ(z1−β)

The same equation, solved for the power term.

Formula for a yes or no predictor

N = [ z1−α/2 √P (1 − P) / R + z1−β √P0 (1 − P0) + P1 (1 − P1) (1 − R) / R ]2(P0 − P1)2 (1 − R) (1 − R2)

P0 and P1 are the chance of the outcome without and with the factor. R is the share of people who have the factor. P = (1 − R) P0 + R P1. This page charts the number-scale formula only.

Source

Hsieh, Bloch and Larsen (1998), as given in the PASS chapter "Logistic Regression (Legacy)" (PDF).

Hsieh FY, Bloch DA, Larsen MD. A simple method of sample size calculation for linear and logistic regression. Stat Med. 1998;17:1623-1634.

What each input does

  • Odds ratio: as the odds ratio increases, fewer people are needed for the same power. A bigger effect is easier to see. See it below
  • Baseline chance of the outcome: the further the chance is below 50%, the more people are needed. A rare outcome gives few cases to learn from. See it below
  • Power: 80%, 85%, 90%, 95%. As we increase power we need more people, because we want to be more confident that a real difference is found. See it below
  • R² with other factors: held at 0. We assume the score is independent of the other factors.
  • Alpha: held at 0.05, two-sided.

The equation

N+ = [ z1−α/2 √V(AUC0) + z1−β √V(AUC1) ]2(AUC1 − AUC0)2

N+ is the number of people with the disease. People without it = R × N+, so the total is N+ × (1 + R).

z1−β = (AUC1 − AUC0) √N+ − z1−α/2 √V(AUC0)√V(AUC1)power = Φ(z1−β)

The same equation, solved for the power term.

For a score on a smooth scale, V uses the Hanley and McNeil variance:

V(θ) = θR (2 − θ) + 2θ21 + θ − θ2 (1 + R)R

One ROC curve tested against a null AUC, as given in the PASS chapter "Tests for One ROC Curve" (PDF).

What each input does

  • Expected AUC: the further the AUC is above 0.50, the fewer people are needed. A better score is easier to tell from a coin flip. See it below
  • People without the disease per person with it: the more there are, the fewer people with the disease are needed. The gain is small past about 10, and the total keeps growing. See it below
  • Power: 80%, 85%, 90%, 95%. As we increase power we need more people, because we want to be more confident that a real difference is found. See it below
  • Null AUC: held at 0.50, a coin flip.
  • Alpha: held at 0.05, two-sided.

These are planning formulas. Treat the answers as a starting point, and add room for dropouts.
