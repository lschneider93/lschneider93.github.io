How many patients does a study need to have a certain power?

Power and sample size calculations are often required in cancer studies, and in medical data in general. The formulas come from the NCSS PASS documentation.

The sample size equationThe power equation

N × P is the number of events that occur.

N × P = (z1−α/2 + z1−β)2(1 − R2) σ2 (ln HR)2⇒N = (z1−α/2 + z1−β)2P (1 − R2) σ2 (ln HR)2

Divide by P to get the number of patients, the sample size.

z1−β = √N · P · (1 − R2) · σ · |ln HR| − z1−α/2⇒power = Φ(z1−β)

The numbers we use come from this paper

Cuzick J, Swanson GP, Fisher G, et al. Prognostic value of an RNA expression signature derived from cell cycle proliferation genes in patients with prostate cancer: a retrospective study. Lancet Oncol. 2011;12(3):245-255. PubMed 21310658 · free full text

  • Hazard ratio 1.89: per 1-point rise in the score.
  • P 0.36: 36% of patients had the cancer return by 10 years.
  • SD 0.70, assumption: the paper gives the middle half of scores, -0.30 to 0.64, but no SD. For a bell-shaped score, SD is about that width divided by 1.35.
  • R² 0, assumption: the 1.89 comes from a model with the score alone.

The sample size equationThe power equation

This form is for a variable on a number scale.

N = (z1−α/2 + z1−β)2P0 (1 − P0) (1 − R2) (ln OR)2

P0 (1 − P0) is largest when P0 is 50%, so that is where the fewest people are needed.

z1−β = √N · P0 (1 − P0) · (1 − R2) · |ln OR| − z1−α/2⇒power = Φ(z1−β)

The numbers we use come from this paper

Mavaddat N, Michailidou K, Dennis J, et al. Polygenic risk scores for prediction of breast cancer and breast cancer subtypes. Am J Hum Genet. 2019;104(1):21-34. PubMed 30554720 · DOI

  • Odds ratio 1.61: per 1 SD rise in the genetic risk score.
  • P0 0.017, assumption: the paper reports 3,215 new breast cancers among 190,040 UK Biobank women, which is 1.7%. Using it as the chance for a woman with an average score is a planning choice.
  • R² 0, assumption: treats the score as unrelated to the other factors in the model.

Formula for a yes or no variable

N = [ z1−α/2 √P (1 − P) / R + z1−β √P0 (1 − P0) + P1 (1 − P1) (1 − R) / R ]2(P0 − P1)2 (1 − R) (1 − R2)

P0 and P1 are the chance of the outcome without and with the factor. R is the share of people who have the factor. P = (1 − R) P0 + R P1. The charts on this page use the number-scale formula only.

The sample size equationThe power equation

N+ is the number of people with the disease.

N+ = [ z1−α/2 √V(AUC0) + z1−β √V(AUC1) ]2(AUC1 − AUC0)2⇒total = N+ (1 + R)

Multiply by 1 + R to get the total number of people.

z1−β = (AUC1 − AUC0) √N+ − z1−α/2 √V(AUC0)√V(AUC1)⇒power = Φ(z1−β)

The numbers we use come from this paper

Rockhill B, Spiegelman D, Byrne C, Hunter DJ, Colditz GA. Validation of the Gail et al. model of breast cancer risk prediction and implications for chemoprevention. J Natl Cancer Inst. 2001;93(5):358-366. PubMed 11238697 · DOI

  • AUC 0.58: reported as the concordance statistic.
  • R 59.6: from 1,354 breast cancer cases among 82,109 women.
  • Null AUC 0.50, assumption: a coin flip. It is the usual "no skill" mark to beat.
  • Smooth-scale variance, assumption: the risk estimate is a number, so the continuous-data formula applies.

The variance term V(θ)

For a variable on a smooth scale, V uses the Hanley and McNeil variance.

V(θ) = θR (2 − θ) + 2θ21 + θ − θ2 (1 + R)R

These are planning formulas. Treat the answers as a starting point, and add room for dropouts.
